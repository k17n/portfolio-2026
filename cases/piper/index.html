Designing an AI co-pilot for the job search

P.I.P.E.R. is a private co-pilot that reads your inbox, drafts your outreach and prepares your applications, and is designed never to act without you. The first module is built and in daily use; the rest are designed or planned.

Role
Product designer and engineer, solo, end to end
Constraints
Fully local, one consumer graphics card, read-only access to a personal inbox

The idea: a co-pilot, not a tracker

A job search is five jobs at once: knowing where you stand, writing to people, telling your story, preparing for conversations, and applying. Today each lives in a different tool, or in your head, and none knows what the others know. A tracker on its own is a spreadsheet with extra steps.

The opportunity is a co-pilot that carries one context across all five: your history with each company, your own strengths and stories, your preferences, used wherever you are in the process.

Pipeline
Reads your inbox and tracks every application. Built, in use.
Writing
Cover letters, LinkedIn, WhatsApp and email drafts grounded in your own material. Designed, full spec.
Résumés
A tailored version per role, from the same knowledge. Planned.
Interviews
Likely questions and your best-matching stories, per company. Planned.
Applying
Prepares an application for a role you choose; you review and send. Planned.

The design question

A co-pilot lives or dies on one question: how much should it do, and how does it earn the right to do more? My answer is a ladder, where each step is earned by being right at the one below.

1 · Observe
Reads and organises. The inbox becomes a pipeline. Built.
2 · Suggest
Proposes with evidence; you decide. Review queue and suggested memories. Built and designed.
3 · Draft
Produces work to review, showing its sources. A cover letter with “claims to check”. Designed.
4 · Prepare
Does the legwork; you press the button. An application ready to review and submit. Planned.
5 · Act alone
Does things for you, unprompted. Ruled out: it never sends or submits.

I deliberately stop at level 4. Everything a person’s name goes on stays a person’s decision.

One shared brain

Three things are shared by every module:

  • The company thread: everything between you and a company, including emails, notes, drafts, and what you actually sent.
  • Your knowledge base: résumé, strengths, stories and past writing you liked, kept as plain files on your machine.
  • Memory: preferences it learns, only with your approval.

The pipeline feeds the thread, writing reads it, applying will too. That’s what makes it a co-pilot rather than four tools: yesterday’s conversation shapes tomorrow’s draft.

Module one, built: the foundation of trust

An assistant that is right nine times in ten and silent about the tenth is worse than a spreadsheet, because you can’t tell which one was wrong. So the first module is about trust.

  • When it’s unsure, it asks. Anything the model doubts becomes a small, answerable question with the email one click away. When an update doesn’t say which application it belongs to, it asks instead of guessing. Every number traces back to its emails. The cost: it isn’t hands-off.
  • Nothing leaves the machine unless I click. The model runs on my own hardware and the app talks only to Gmail, read-only. The cost: setup friction, the first thing I’d redesign.
  • “Ghosted” is a suggestion, not a verdict. Conclusions are always derived, so they can be overridden, undone and improved without breaking history.
  • It covers what email can’t see. Referrals and phone screens go in through light manual capture that later emails merge into.

Where the co-pilot goes next

Modules two to five aren’t built yet. The writing module is specified end to end in the Writing Studio PRD, and the others are on the roadmap. Here is what each one adds.

  • Writing drafts a cover letter, LinkedIn note, WhatsApp message or email from your own knowledge base and the company thread. It picks the format for the channel and asks when that’s unclear (output formats). Each draft has to use something specific to the company and to you, and is checked against your earlier drafts so letters don’t converge on a template (uniqueness and quality). Every claim about you is tied to a source, and anything without one is flagged “claims to check”. What it adds: tailoring that takes 20 to 40 minutes by hand becomes a first draft to edit, and the interface makes invention visible instead of hoping the model doesn’t invent.
  • Memory across sessions keeps each conversation as a thread per company (threads). Style preferences and facts are proposed as suggestions, approved in one click, and editable (learning across sessions). What it adds: a co-pilot that gets better at writing as you, with nothing learned silently.
  • Résumés and interview prep reuse the same knowledge base and company thread: a tailored résumé per role, and likely questions with your best-matching stories per company. What it adds: the work done for the pipeline and for writing carries over, so none of it is entered twice.
  • Applying is the riskiest module, because it acts in the world. It prepares an application for a role you choose, with the tailored résumé, cover letter and answers ready to review, and records it in the pipeline. What it adds: the legwork is gone, and pressing submit stays your decision.

How I tested the thinking

I ran the first module on six months of my own inbox. Three findings changed the product.

  • Identical confirmation emails merged into one application, so the principle became: when the system can’t tell, it asks.
  • A rejection went undetected because my search assumed the shape of a job-board message.
  • The review queue only works if it stays small. I tuned thresholds until about 1% of emails reached me.

Impact so far

This is module one and one user, so it shows whether it works, not market adoption.

Decisions
About 1% of the ~390 emails needed a decision from me
Blind spot
Nearly one in four applications are ones a job-board tracker can’t see

What I’d build and measure next

Build the writing module end to end, then put it in front of five job-seekers. A co-pilot succeeds when people send what it wrote and edit it less each time. So I’d measure edit distance from draft to final, the share of drafts actually used, claims flagged per draft, and time to a usable first draft.

What I’d change

  • The first run. Setup is too heavy for anyone but a power user.
  • The evidence. Tested on one person so far.
  • Accessibility. I had Claude audit the interface by reading the code and measuring WCAG colour contrast. It found contrast and keyboard gaps that I haven’t fixed yet, and I haven’t tested with a screen reader.
